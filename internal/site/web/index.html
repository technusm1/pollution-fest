Festival highlights: what you get, whether you bought a ticket or not 🎟️

From mid-October to late November, farm smoke rolls across the Indo-Gangetic plain, meets cold still air, and settles in for the season. Delhi's official AQI (CPCB 24-hour average) was 376 at 4 PM on Diwali, Sun 8 Nov 2026, and 344 at 4 PM on Mon 9 Nov, with 15 stations in the "severe" band. On Diwali night, PM2.5 readings at several monitoring stations conveniently went blank. Here's this year's programme.

⏳

Complimentary life-shortening

If Delhi's air met the WHO guideline, residents would live about 8.2 years longer. Haryana: 5.3 years. UP: 5 years. Year-round dirty air does this, and the festival is its loudest act.

AQLI 2025, EPIC Chicago (2023 data)

🎤

The headliner: stubble smoke

On the worst days, farm fires made up 38% of Delhi's PM2.5 in 2024 and 22% in 2025. Across November it averages less (20% in 2024, 7% in 2025), but it turns bad air into a public-health emergency overnight.

IITM Decision Support System, via CREA (Nov 2025 snapshot)

🥁

The encore nobody asked for

When the fires stop, Delhi's December smog often gets worse, thanks to its own vehicles, dust and industry. Stubble is the guest who gets blamed for the whole party, then leaves early while the party carries on.

CSE analysis, Dec 2025

🏫

Surprise holidays!

Schools go online. Construction stops. This year: a 15-day construction ban across NCR from 5 Nov, and half of office staff in Noida and Gurugram sent home. Construction workers get the holiday too. The pay is another matter.

Indian Express, Nov 2026

🌾

Roasted soil, served hot

Every tonne of straw burnt sends roughly 5.5 kg of nitrogen, 25 kg of potash, 1.2 kg of sulphur and ~400 kg of carbon up in smoke. Farmers then buy fertiliser to put it back. Truly a circular economy.

Govt. of Goa / Frontiers in Soil Science, 2025

🏡

Front-row seats: rural Punjab & Haryana

Delhi gets the headlines, but the villages next to the burning fields breathe it first and worst, with no smog towers and no TV debates. They're also where the matchstick gets lit, which tells you how little choice is involved.

Hide and seek, national championship edition

The satellites behind official fire counts circle the Earth on a fixed schedule. Over Punjab, the main ones pass over between noon and 3 PM, then come back at 1–3 AM.

Farmers worked this out. In 2024 the Supreme Court heard that farmers were allegedly being advised to burn after the satellites had gone. iFOREST found over 90% of large fires in 2024–25 burned after 3 PM. In 2025 an ISRO study confirmed that peak burning had shifted to late afternoon and evening.

So why does the chart below show any afternoon fires at all? Thank Aqua, a NASA satellite launched in 2002. In retirement its orbit is drifting later in the day, and it now limps over Punjab at around 3–4:30 PM. Our best eye on the evening is a 24-year-old satellite that's running late. 🛰️👴

Before you blame the farmer: a quick maths lesson 🧮

It's late October in Sangrur. The paddy is cut. Wheat has to be sown in about two weeks or the yield suffers. The field is covered in loose straw. Our farmer has these options:

🌱 Rent a seeder to sow straight into the strawThousands of rupees an acre. Punjab reckoned ₹2,500/acre of compensation was fair.Whenever the machine turns up3.54 lakh subsidised machines exist across four states, and every farmer needs one in the same fortnight.

Put any of us in those boots and we'd be lighting the match too. Burning isn't a character flaw. It's the cheapest answer to a problem built by people who will never stand in that field.

Why the government fines the farmer instead of fixing anything

✅ Easy: one press release, done by lunch

  • Double the fines
  • File FIRs against farmers
  • Put "red entries" in their land records
  • Blame the neighbouring state (ruled by the other party, obviously)
  • Inaugurate a smog tower; pose for photos
  • Form a committee

🏗️ Hard: takes longer than one election cycle

  • Change what Punjab grows. Guaranteed government buying (MSP) makes thirsty paddy the safe bet, so Punjab grows rice that mostly leaves the state, using groundwater it doesn't have.
  • Fix the crop calendar squeezed by the 2009 groundwater law, without draining the aquifers.
  • Build a real straw market (collection, storage, buyers, prices) so straw is worth more than a matchstick.
  • Get machines to small farmers on time, not just on paper.
  • Pay farmers not to burn, and actually send the money.
  • Make the Centre and five state governments, run by rival parties, cooperate for more than one press conference.

A fine fits in a tweet and makes the problem the farmer's fault. A fix takes years, money and cooperation between rivals, and the credit might go to whoever wins the next election. So, year after year, we get the tweet.

So: Punjab drains its aquifers to grow rice the country already has too much of, while India spends lakhs of crores importing oil and pulses that Punjab could grow on far less water. Somewhere, a committee is calling this "food security".

Why farmers still pick paddy (spoiler: they're not stupid)

  • The government buys every grain of it at the support price (MSP). Almost no other crop gets that guarantee.
  • Pumping water is basically free, thanks to free farm power, so the aquifer's bill goes to the next generation.
  • The alternatives sell for far less than promised in the mandi. Maize sold for as little as ₹800/quintal in Punjab against an MSP of ₹1,850, with hardly any government buying. In 2022, summer moong sold below its ₹7,275 MSP and the CM promised to cover up to ₹1,000/quintal of the gap; growers said the MSP became the ceiling price. An MSP is just a number when nobody buys at it.

How to actually make them switch (hint: not a press release)

💰 Things that would work

  • Guarantee the price of the alternative. Buy maize, pulses and oilseeds at MSP, or pay the gap when the mandi pays less.
  • Pay for water saved, whatever the crop. Fund it from the power subsidy the farmer would stop using.
  • Line up anchor buyers. Ethanol and feed plants contract maize at a fixed price before sowing, like the government does for paddy.
  • Keep paddy where the water allows, but short-duration and direct-seeded.

🧪 What's been tried

  • Punjab's 2025 pilot paid ₹17,500/hectare and promised to buy maize at ₹2,400/quintal.
  • Kharif maize rose from 86,000 to ~1 lakh hectares, and the scheme grew to 16 districts in 2026-27.
  • Paddy covers ~30 lakh hectares. So: a promising start, and also a rounding error.

The pattern isn't complicated: farmers grow whatever the government guarantees to buy. Today that's paddy. So we get paddy, sinking aquifers and smoke, and then an emergency meeting about the smoke. Fixing it means guaranteeing a market for pulses, oilseeds and maize, and that costs real money for years. A fine costs nothing.

The fine print (we're the honest ones here)

  • Detections, not fires. Each point is a 375 m (VIIRS) or 1 km (MODIS) pixel that one satellite flagged as hot during one pass. The same field can show up on several satellites.
  • Undercount by design. Polar satellites look a few times a day. A falling count can mean better hiding, not less burning.
  • Burnt area is the real measure. India still has no finalised burnt-area protocol, two years after the Supreme Court asked (Indian Express, Nov 2026). Burnt-area mapping is on this project's roadmap.
  • Not every fire is stubble. Industrial heat sources are removed where FIRMS flags them (archived data). Recent data may include a few.
  • Season comparison uses one satellite (NOAA-20), because S-NPP stopped sending data on 2 Nov 2026 and NOAA-21 only started in 2023. Mixing them would fake a trend.
  • Boundaries are Census-2011 districts; newer districts are counted inside their parent.
